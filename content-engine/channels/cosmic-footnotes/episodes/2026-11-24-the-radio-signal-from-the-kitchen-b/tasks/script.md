# Skill: write-script

You receive a task file with the channel, the series, its characters, the channel's learnings, the idea, the
research dossier (`research.json`: the facts with their sources and quotes, the people, the events in order with
their whys, the turn, the key objects, and what could not be verified), and the SKILL.md of every render skill the
series uses. You write **one file: `script.json`**. Nothing else.

## How to write it

0. **Find the point before you write a line.** Facts told well are a report; people follow a point of view. Fill
   `angle` first:
   - `point`: the one sentence this video argues. It is the channel's **Belief** (channel.md), proved again by this
     story. "The French judges didn't lose to California, they lost to their own labels" — not "a 1976 tasting".
   - `stakes`: who in the story has something to lose, and exactly what, in plain words a stranger can feel ("her
     name rested on French wine being the best", not "an important editor"). Tell the story through them, never
     as a list of events.
   - `turn`: the moment the facts change meaning. Build the middle so the viewer walks into it.
   - `takeaway`: what the viewer understands once the story is told (**What the viewer gets** in channel.md). It is
     for you, not a line to say: the story makes it plain on its own. The last beat ends on what happened, the
     result or the one fact that settles it ("they all described the white wine as red"), in plain words. Never
     tell the viewer what to do, feel or believe: no advice, no "next time…", no "trust yourself", no "your taste
     is enough", no moral or summary of what it all means.
   Every beat serves the point: a fact that doesn't move the story toward the turn or the takeaway is cut, however
   true. The angle is opinion and framing; anything factual inside it is a claim like any other.

   **Tell it plainly, in the order it happened.** You are explaining something simply to a friend, not
   performing. Say what was going on, what everyone expected, what happened, and what came of it, in that order.
   Don't open on the ending, tease, flash back, or add dramatic lines ("so here's the thing", "and then?",
   questions to the viewer mid-story). If a line sounds clever, say the plain thing instead. Every person, thing
   and place is introduced before it is referred to, and when someone does something surprising, say why in
   plain words ("she asked for her sheet back, so no one would see she'd picked California").
1. **Follow the series.** Use its beats, in order, with the same ids. Each beat's `purpose` says what it must do;
   `seconds` is a target — the voice decides the real timing.
2. **Hook first.** The first sentence of the first beat is ~5 words and lands within 2 seconds. The captions put it
   on screen from the first frame; there is no headline, so don't write `on_screen_text`. Surprising claims work;
   greetings don't.
   The hook is a plain true statement of what happened that is surprising on its own ("France lost at wine."),
   complete for a stranger: no "the sheet" or "he" for something not yet introduced. It is not a tease; the rest
   of the video tells how it happened, in order.
3. **Write for the ear.** Short sentences. Contractions. One idea per sentence. Numbers the way people say them.
   Read it out loud in your head at the character's speed. Total words are given in the task — stay inside them.
   **A full stop ends a thought.** The voice drops its pitch and pauses at every `.` `!` `?`, so never cut one
   phrase into pieces: "You think all wine gets better with time?" — not "You think all wine improves. With
   time." Fragments that hang off the sentence before ("With time." "Mere weeks after harvest." "On purpose.")
   join it with a comma or a dash. For a beat of drama inside a line, use a comma, a dash or "…", which pause
   without ending the thought. A one-word sentence only when it's a full reply on its own ("Wrong.").
4. **Be the character.** Use its personality, vocabulary and verbal tics from its file. A catchphrase at most once.
5. **Show, don't decorate.** Every beat has a visual that explains what is being said (see the render skills).
   Never make a shot whose subject is writing (a page, score sheet, letter, sign, label or screen): the picture
   model can't draw readable text, so it comes out as scribbles. Show the person doing something with it instead
   (reading it with the paper edge-on, reaching for it).
6. **Facts are claims, from the dossier.** Write only from `research.json`: its facts, its people, its order and its
   whys. Every fact — spoken, on screen, in a part label, or a number in a graphic — goes into `claims` with the
   `source` of the dossier fact it comes from, and says no more than that fact's `quote` supports. Never use
   anything in its `unverified` list. A fact the dossier doesn't have is cut, however true. (An older episode with no
   dossier: a real source you have checked. Unverifiable → cut it. Confidence `low` → find a better source.)
7. **Pronunciations.** If the voice may stumble (years, acronyms, names, symbols), add
   `"pronunciations": {"1938": "nineteen thirty-eight", "LED": "L E D"}`. Captions still show the original word.
8. **No AI-label text, no links, no "subscribe", no call to action.** A series' `cta` is a fixed closing line, never advice.

# Skill: render-storybook

A scene skill: during the beats that use it, it fills the frame with a square illustrated card (full width, from
y≈420 to y≈1500) and a title bar above it. You never draw pixels — you **describe scenes** from a fixed library of
settings, poses and props, and the skill draws them with wobbly hand-drawn lines, muted watercolour washes and paper
grain. Same script → same picture (fixed seeds), so re-renders are stable.

It suits stories told one picture at a time: a narrator character acting out a metaphor ("if you took one step
every…"), each sentence its own scene. Pair it with the `captions` skill and `caption_style: single-word` in the
series (one word at a time in the middle of the picture) and `skills.render: [render-storybook]` (no separate
character layer — the character lives inside the scenes).

## Title bar: `visuals["render-storybook"]`

```jsonc
{
  "title": "A billion seconds is how long?",  // 3–64 chars, shown on top for the whole video, upper-cased
  "highlight": ["billion seconds"]             // ≤ 4 parts of the title drawn in the channel's accent colour
}
```

Keep titles **≤ 30 characters** so they fit on one line at a readable size (longer titles wrap into two balanced
lines and get smaller). The title is on screen the whole time → its words and numbers are claims too.

## Each beat: `visual`

```jsonc
{
  "skill": "render-storybook",

}
```

**Timing is automatic:** scene 1 starts with the beat, scene 2 with the beat's second sentence, and so on (a short
cross-fade between scenes). More scenes than sentences split the last sentence evenly. Fewer scenes than sentences
keep the last scene up. So write the voiceover in short sentences and give each one a picture.

## Layout rules (read before placing anything)

- The picture is a 1000×1000 grid in scene units; the ground line is at about y = 830 (845–860 on beach, sea,
  room and city).
- **Caption band:** single-word captions appear at scene y ≈ 330–390, horizontally centred. Keep faces and
  lettering out of that band: standing characters at `scale` ≤ 1.2 (normal shots) or ≥ 1.9 (close-ups, the face is
  above the band) — scales in between put the caption across the face.
- Sky lettering (`text`, `speech`, `thought`) goes at y ≤ 0.28. Keep everything inside 0.05–0.95: the picture's
  edges are darkened by a vignette.
- One focal point per scene: the character plus 1–3 props. Leave air around the character.
- Every scene is a new picture: change the setting, the camera, the pose or the props from one sentence to the
  next. Escalate — the story's scenes should get bigger (one jar → a row of jars → the ocean).

## Writing for this skill

- The hook beat's first scene shows the number itself (`sign`, `text` or `calendar`) with the character reacting.
- Numbers on props are exactly the numbers in the voiceover ("1,000,000,000" ↔ "a billion"), rounded the same way.
- Every word on a prop (`text`, `hold_text`) and the title are on-screen text: factual ones need a claim with a
  source in `claims`, and the fact-check covers them.
- Short lettering: props ≤ 28 characters, held signs ≤ 12. Use digits with commas for big numbers.

an `arrow`/`footprints` has no `x2`, a `sign`/`calendar`/`speech`/`text` has no `text`, a beat has many more scenes
than sentences, or an `expression` isn't in the speaker's model.json.

# Skill: captions

Captions are generated, not written: you don't add anything to the script for them. They show the voiceover
exactly as written in `script.json` (so spelling and capitalisation there are what viewers read), a short page at a
time (≤ 2 lines), and highlight each word while it is spoken.

## How it works

- **Timings** come from the voice step (`audio/timings.json`). The local voice (Kokoro) predicts how long every
  phoneme lasts before generating audio; the engine reads those durations, so each word's start/end is exact — no
  speech recognition, no drift.
- **Pages** break at sentence ends and beat boundaries, and when a line would get too wide for the safe area.
- **Style**: white words with a dark outline, the spoken one on the channel's `accent_color`, in the channel's
  `font` and `caption_case` (`upper` for ALL-CAPS captions). Captions sit in the lower part of the safe area, above
  platform UI. A video's own settings (`captions.json`, else its storyboard's) can change the font, the words'
  colour, the highlight, the size, the place and the case, and hide them on some beats.
- **Drawn last, on their own**: the render makes the video without captions first (`out/<slug>/clean/<episode>.mp4`),
  then draws the captions over it. Changing how they look (the storefront's captions step, seen live over the clean
  video) draws only the captions again, in under a minute; nothing else is made again.

## What this means for the script writer

- Write numbers and names the way they should appear on screen; add `pronunciations` if the voice should say
  them differently (`"1938": "nineteen thirty-eight"`).
- Very long words (14+ letters) get a line to themselves; prefer shorter words.
- Punctuation shows on screen. Use "—" and "…" sparingly; they also create pauses in the voice.

## QA

`workflows/qa.md` checks that caption words equal the script, the highlighted word matches the timings on ≥ 98 %
of frames, no word is shown over silence, and captions never leave the safe area or overflow.
</file>
